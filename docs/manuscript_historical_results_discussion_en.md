## 3. Results

### 3.1. Historical trend in Kazakhstan’s IPPU emissions

Kazakhstan’s total IPPU emissions, as reported in the 2025 Common Reporting Tables (CRT), declined sharply during the early transition period and subsequently recovered (Figure 1). Total emissions decreased from 22,741 kt CO2eq in 1990 to a series minimum of 12,258 kt CO2eq in 1996, a reduction of 46.1%. They then increased to 26,773 kt CO2eq in 2023, equivalent to a 118.4% increase from the 1996 minimum and 17.7% above the 1990 level. The temporal pattern indicates that the post-Soviet industrial contraction affected IPPU activity substantially, whereas the later recovery did not restore the original category composition.

The series is based on the total IPPU value reported in Kazakhstan CRT 2025 Table 2(I), expressed using AR5 global-warming potentials (GWPs). It should therefore be interpreted as the official inventory trajectory, rather than as a direct measure of physical industrial output. Changes in emissions can reflect changes in output, technology, product composition, activity-data sources and inventory methodology.

### 3.2. Changes in IPPU emissions composition

Metal industry (IPCC 2.C) remained the largest component of IPPU emissions throughout the period, although its relative contribution declined from 81.6% in 1990 to 50.4% in 2023 (Figure 2). In 2023, metal industry emitted 13,486 kt CO2eq. Mineral industry (2.A) increased from 12.8% of total IPPU emissions in 1990 to 36.0% in 2023, reaching 9,637 kt CO2eq. The increasing importance of mineral industry is consistent with the significance of clinker-based cement production and other carbonate-related processes in the current inventory.

F-gases from product uses as substitutes for ozone-depleting substances (2.F) constituted 10.2% of IPPU emissions in 2023, or 2,722 kt CO2eq. This category was negligible in the early years of the series but has become material for the current IPPU profile. Chemical industry (2.B) accounted for 1.9%, while non-energy fuel and solvent use (2.D) accounted for 1.5%. Thus, Kazakhstan’s contemporary IPPU profile cannot be represented only by metallurgical activity: mineral processes and F-gases must be retained as distinct branches in any future sectoral model.

### 3.3. Comparison of CRF 2023 and CRT 2025 submissions

The comparison of the 2023 Common Reporting Format (CRF) and 2025 CRT submissions does not support the earlier assumption that the newer submission is approximately 10% higher in 2020–2021. For total IPPU emissions, CRT 2025 was 31.915 kt CO2eq (0.118%) lower than CRF 2023 in 2020 and 530.264 kt CO2eq (1.958%) lower in 2021. The corresponding CO2-only differences were +0.872% in 2020 and −1.231% in 2021.

These values should not be interpreted as pure inventory revisions. CRF 2023 CO2eq totals were recalculated from component gases using AR4 GWPs, whereas CRT 2025 reports totals using AR5 GWPs. The CO2-only comparison is less affected by the GWP update, but it still cannot identify the causal source of differences without category-specific methodological evidence. At category level, the 2021 total CO2eq difference was concentrated in mineral industry: 2.A was 468.084 kt CO2eq lower in CRT 2025 than in CRF 2023. In contrast, metal industry differed by only 4.139 kt CO2eq (+0.028%). This pattern motivates targeted review of mineral-industry methods rather than a general claim of increasing uncertainty in the whole inventory.

### 3.4. Activity-data comparability at process level

The 2021 BNS–CRT comparison demonstrates that numerical agreement depends on whether indicators have the same product definition and reporting boundary (Table 1). Cement clinker, aggregate lime, crude steel and total ferroalloy output match exactly between BNS and CRT. For clinker, both sources report 7.295700 Mt; for lime, 0.933623 Mt; for crude steel, 4.526071 Mt; and for ferroalloys, 2.070038 Mt. These matches validate the corresponding indicators as baseline activity-data candidates, while not eliminating the need for process-specific emission factors and technology parameters.

Two apparent differences are not valid evidence of poor data quality. First, BNS reports 12.312700 Mt of Portland cement, whereas CRT reports 7.295700 Mt of clinker. The resulting −40.7% difference is a cross-product comparison. NID 2025 confirms that the inventory uses BNS clinker production, not Portland-cement output, for category 2.A.1. Second, BNS reports 0.300886 Mt of total unwrought zinc, while CRT category 2.C.6 reports 0.081861 Mt of zinc produced from Waelz cakes in Waelz kilns. NID 2025 identifies the latter as a Kazzinc technological stream; the apparent −72.8% difference therefore compares a national aggregate with a narrow process stream.

The pig-iron comparison also requires a boundary qualification. BNS reports 3.623764 Mt of pig iron in 2021, while CRT reports 3.195305 Mt. NID 2025 indicates that the inventory calculation relies on Qarmet integrated-plant information, including coke, limestone and carbon-balance inputs. The 11.8% difference should consequently not be treated as an established inconsistency between BNS and UNFCCC. For primary aluminium, the available BNS long series combines unwrought aluminium and aluminium oxide, while the inventory relies on Kazakhstan Electrolysis Plant data, including anode and anode-effect information. No valid like-for-like comparison can currently be made for this category.

### 3.5. LEAP IPPU process-emissions pathways, 2024–2050

LEAP pathways were calculated from a 2024 Current Accounts calibration of selected process-emission branches. The boundary includes mineral-industry, metal-industry and chemical-industry processes, but excludes F-gases, aluminium PFCs, several minor IPPU sources and emissions from industrial fuel combustion. The 2024 selected-boundary total is 23,552 kt CO2eq; it is therefore not a complete-sector projection.

The Baseline holds each included flow constant from 2024 through 2050. Under Moderate mitigation, aggregate included emissions decline to 20,715 kt CO2eq in 2030 and 14,827 kt CO2eq in 2050, reductions of 2,837 and 8,725 kt CO2eq relative to Baseline. Under Ambitious mitigation, they decline to 17,157 kt CO2eq in 2030 and 7,736 kt CO2eq in 2050, reductions of 6,395 and 15,816 kt CO2eq, respectively (Figure 4). The results reflect linear emissions multipliers by broad process group, rather than an endogenous representation of output growth, individual technologies, capital turnover or cost. The factors represent stylised portfolios of recognised measures: material/clinker substitution and residual-emission capture for mineral processes; circular-material, reduction-route and capture measures for metals; and optimisation, N2O abatement, route change and residual-emission control for chemicals. They are author-selected scenario envelopes, not technology-specific potentials or official national targets.

The decomposition of avoided emissions shows that metal-industry processes contribute the largest absolute reduction in both mitigation cases, followed by mineral-industry processes (Figure 5). This is a consequence of their larger 2024 calibrated emissions within the model boundary and should not be interpreted as evidence that any particular technology or policy is more feasible in Kazakhstan.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. The contribution of process-boundary harmonisation

The principal methodological finding is that a percentage difference between BNS and UNFCCC values is not, by itself, a measure of activity-data quality. This is particularly important in IPPU, where a category can represent a chemical transformation, an integrated industrial route or a specific sub-process rather than the marketed product reported in national statistics. The clinker and zinc cases demonstrate opposite forms of the same problem. In the clinker case, an inappropriate substitution of Portland cement for clinker creates a large artificial difference despite exact agreement for the appropriate activity indicator. In the zinc case, an apparently large difference results from comparing total national output with a specific Waelz-kiln stream.

This finding has direct implications for the interpretation of inventory uncertainty. It would be incorrect to rank data quality solely by the absolute BNS–UNFCCC difference and to conclude that zinc or cement data are necessarily unreliable. A more defensible workflow first establishes the identity of the physical product, geographical coverage, plant boundary, technological route and unit of measurement. Only the residual difference between genuinely equivalent indicators can be investigated as a possible inconsistency. This workflow is consistent with the need for technology-rich industrial modelling identified in the LEAP, cement and steel literature.

### 4.2. Implications for LEAP calibration and scenario interpretation

The historical analysis provides the calibration logic for the current scenario implementation. For cement, clinker is the appropriate baseline activity indicator for process emissions; Portland-cement output can be retained as contextual information or for a later clinker-to-cement-ratio scenario. For lime, the national aggregate may be used in a base year, while the 85/15 allocation between high-calcium and dolomitic lime applied in the inventory must be made explicit. For steel and ferroalloys, total production validates an output indicator, but the emission calculation must also represent reductant consumption, carbon content, scrap use or alloy composition where these are material.

Pig iron, primary aluminium and zinc require more cautious treatment. Pig-iron emissions cannot be calibrated solely to the wider BNS product series when the inventory uses Qarmet-specific carbon-balance data. Aluminium requires plant-level or disaggregated production data because the available BNS series merges aluminium and alumina. Zinc should be represented as the Waelz-kiln process reported in the inventory, not as national unwrought-zinc output. These distinctions determine the future LEAP model tree and prevent double counting or false calibration.

The observed change in IPPU composition also matters for model scope. A Kazakhstan IPPU model that includes only cement and metals would omit a material and growing F-gas component. Conversely, energy-combustion emissions from industry should not be added to IPPU process emissions unless they are explicitly modelled in a linked energy module, because the national inventory assigns those emissions to the Energy sector.

### 4.3. Limitations

Several limitations should qualify the findings. First, the CRF–CRT CO2eq comparison spans different GWP frameworks; it can indicate where methodological investigation is required but cannot isolate inventory revision effects. Second, BNS long series are not yet available in a directly comparable form for all priority products, particularly clinker and steel. Third, the study relies on publicly available inventory documentation and cannot independently audit confidential plant data. Fourth, the exploratory pathways use exogenous emissions multipliers and a flat baseline. The measure narratives establish why progressively deeper cases are relevant, but the numerical factors have not been estimated from plant-level Kazakhstan technology, cost or diffusion data. They quantify the scale implied by stated assumptions, rather than technology-specific mitigation potential, cost, investment or policy feasibility.

The model was executed in LEAP 2026.5.0.1 after correcting the CH4 and N2O input units. Subsequent work should test alternative activity-data assumptions, technology pathways and emission factors. The current results should be interpreted as explicitly bounded sensitivity pathways, not as a quantified forecast of the entire IPPU sector.
