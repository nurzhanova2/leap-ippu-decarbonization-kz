# Speaker script: IAMC 2026 oral presentation

Русская рабочая версия: [presentation_speaker_script.md](presentation_speaker_script.md).

**Presentation:** *LEAP-Based modeling of Decarbonization pathways in the IPPU sector of Kazakhstan*  
**Format:** 15 slides; approximately 11–12 minutes at a calm pace.  
**Audience:** IAMC researchers and practitioners.  
**Rule:** Use the wording below as speaking guidance, not a text to read word-for-word. Pause after the main numerical results and let the figures remain visible.

## Slide-by-slide speech

### Slide 1 — Title | 0:00–0:25

Good morning, everyone. My name is Zarina Nurzhanova from Astana IT University. Today I will present our work on LEAP-based modelling of decarbonisation pathways in Kazakhstan’s Industrial Processes and Product Use, or IPPU, sector. The study combines historical inventory analysis, activity-data harmonisation and exploratory LEAP scenarios. Its central message is simple: credible industrial scenarios begin with credible and comparable process data.

### Slide 2 — Why IPPU matters in Kazakhstan | 0:25–1:05

IPPU emissions are especially relevant for Kazakhstan because its economy includes emissions-intensive mineral and metal industries. These emissions are different from emissions caused by burning fuel in industry: IPPU covers emissions generated by physical or chemical transformations, such as calcination or metallurgical reduction. For bottom-up modelling, activity data are therefore critical. However, official inventories, national production statistics and industrial-process boundaries do not always describe the same physical activity. Before projecting emissions, we need to reconcile these definitions.

### Slide 3 — Research gap, aim and questions | 1:05–1:45

The research gap is that official inventory data are often used in modelling without first checking whether they are consistent with process-level activity indicators. Our aim was to provide a transparent assessment of Kazakhstan’s IPPU emissions from 1990 to 2023, and then use this evidence as a basis for exploratory LEAP scenarios from 2024 to 2050. We ask four questions: how emissions changed over time; how the category composition changed; whether BNS and UNFCCC activity data are comparable; and what transparent mitigation assumptions imply within a selected process-emissions boundary.

### Slide 4 — Literature review and positioning | 1:45–2:25

The literature has three useful strands. Kazakhstan-wide energy and decarbonisation studies provide important economy-wide context, but generally do not reconcile UNFCCC inventory data with industrial activity data at process level. LEAP applications in cement and steel demonstrate how bottom-up technology-oriented scenarios can be built. Finally, industrial-decarbonisation studies show that deep reductions require portfolios of measures beyond energy efficiency alone. Our contribution is to connect these strands: we build a Kazakhstan-specific bridge from official inventory reporting to a transparent, reproducible IPPU scenario model.

### Slide 5 — Data and analytical workflow | 2:25–3:05

We combined five evidence sources: the UNFCCC CRT 2025 submission, the earlier CRF 2023 submission, Bureau of National Statistics production data, Kazakhstan’s NID and NIR documentation, and IPCC methodological guidance. The workflow has five steps. First, we reconstruct historical IPPU emissions. Second, we compare CRF and CRT versions with explicit attention to AR4 and AR5 global-warming-potential differences. Third, we test activity-data comparability process by process. Fourth, we calibrate selected branches in LEAP for 2024. Finally, we calculate Baseline, Moderate and Ambitious exploratory pathways to 2050.

### Slide 6 — Historical IPPU emissions | 3:05–3:55

The historical trajectory answers our first question. Kazakhstan’s reported IPPU emissions declined from 22,741 kilotonnes of CO2 equivalent in 1990 to 12,258 kilotonnes in 1996. This is a decline of about 46 percent and is consistent with the transition-period industrial contraction. Emissions then recovered to 26,773 kilotonnes in 2023, above the 1990 level. This figure should be interpreted as an official inventory trajectory, not as a direct measure of physical industrial output, because it can also reflect technology, product mix, activity-data sources and methodological updates.

### Slide 7 — Changing IPPU composition | 3:55–4:40

The composition of IPPU emissions also changed. In 2023, metal industry was the largest source, at 13,486 kilotonnes of CO2 equivalent, or 50.4 percent. Mineral industry contributed 9,637 kilotonnes, or 36 percent. F-gases accounted for 10.2 percent, while chemical industry and non-energy fuel and solvent use were smaller. The key implication is that Kazakhstan’s current IPPU profile cannot be represented only by cement and metals. Mineral processes and F-gases are both material. In this first LEAP implementation, however, F-gases are outside the selected scenario boundary; this distinction is important for interpreting the results.

### Slide 8 — CRF 2023 versus CRT 2025 | 4:40–5:25

Before drawing conclusions about data quality, we compared the two inventory reporting cycles. The newer CRT submission does not support an earlier assumption of an approximately ten-percent increase in 2020 and 2021. Total IPPU CO2 equivalent in CRT 2025 was 0.118 percent lower in 2020 and 1.958 percent lower in 2021 than in CRF 2023. But this comparison must be handled carefully: CRF 2023 uses AR4 global-warming potentials, whereas CRT 2025 uses AR5. The main 2021 category-level difference occurred in mineral industry, while the metal-industry difference was small. Therefore, differences should guide targeted methodological review, rather than a generic claim that inventory quality has deteriorated.

### Slide 9 — Activity-data comparability | 5:25–6:30

This is the central methodological result. For 2021, clinker, aggregate lime, crude steel and total ferroalloy output match exactly between BNS and CRT data. These indicators are directly usable as output-based starting points, although process-specific factors are still needed. In contrast, apparent differences for pig iron and zinc are not direct evidence of inconsistency. The pig-iron comparison contrasts national BNS output with a Qarmet-specific inventory input. For zinc, national unwrought-zinc production is compared with a narrow Waelz-kiln process stream. Similarly, Portland cement must not be compared directly with clinker. The lesson is that product definition, geographical coverage, technological route, plant boundary and units must be checked before interpreting a numerical difference as uncertainty.

### Slide 10 — LEAP model structure and boundary | 6:30–7:15

Based on that assessment, we implemented a selected process-emissions boundary in LEAP version 2026.5.0.1. It includes mineral-industry processes, metal-industry processes and selected chemical-industry processes. The model excludes F-gases, aluminium PFCs, lead and other minor IPPU sources, and industrial fuel-combustion emissions. The Current Accounts year is 2024, the end year is 2050, and the model uses AR5 global-warming potentials: one for CO2, 28 for methane and 265 for nitrous oxide. The 2024 selected-boundary baseline is 23,552 kilotonnes of CO2 equivalent. It is not the total national IPPU inventory.

### Slide 11 — Exploratory scenario design | 7:15–8:15

We use three cases. Baseline holds every included 2024 process-emission flow constant. Moderate and Ambitious cases linearly interpolate to group-specific remaining-emissions factors in 2030 and 2050. For example, the Moderate case leaves 60 percent of 2024 mineral-process emissions in 2050, while the Ambitious case leaves 30 percent. The factors represent progressively deeper portfolios of measures: material and clinker substitution, process improvements and residual-emission capture for mineral processes; circular-material use, alternative reduction routes and capture for metals; and optimisation, N2O abatement, route change and residual-emission control for chemicals. These are author-selected sensitivity assumptions. They are not official targets, least-cost pathways or technology-specific mitigation potentials.

### Slide 12 — Scenario pathways to 2050 | 8:15–9:10

Under the flat Baseline, included process emissions remain at 23,552 kilotonnes of CO2 equivalent. Under Moderate mitigation, they fall to 20,715 kilotonnes in 2030 and 14,827 kilotonnes in 2050. This is a reduction of 2,837 and 8,725 kilotonnes relative to Baseline. Under Ambitious mitigation, emissions fall to 17,157 kilotonnes in 2030 and 7,736 kilotonnes in 2050, avoiding 6,395 and 15,816 kilotonnes respectively. These are results within the explicitly selected boundary, using the stated assumptions and AR5 GWPs.

### Slide 13 — Avoided emissions by process group | 9:10–9:45

The largest absolute avoided emissions come from metal-industry processes, followed by mineral-industry processes. This is primarily a scale effect: these groups have the largest calibrated emissions within the model boundary. It should not be interpreted as proof that a particular technology, or a particular sector, is necessarily easier to decarbonise in Kazakhstan. The current model quantifies scenario consequences; it does not rank implementation feasibility.

### Slide 14 — Main conclusions | 9:45–10:35

Let me summarise the four conclusions. First, Kazakhstan’s IPPU emissions declined sharply in the 1990s and recovered to 26,773 kilotonnes of CO2 equivalent in 2023. Second, metal and mineral processes dominate the current profile, and F-gases have become material. Third, apparent BNS–UNFCCC differences must be interpreted through physical product and process boundaries, not percentages alone. Fourth, within our selected LEAP boundary, exploratory mitigation pathways reduce emissions to between 14,827 and 7,736 kilotonnes of CO2 equivalent by 2050, compared with a flat baseline of 23,552 kilotonnes.

### Slide 15 — Limitations and outlook | 10:35–11:25

Finally, the limitations are central to the interpretation. The CRF–CRT comparison spans different AR4 and AR5 GWP frameworks. Some activity data are not publicly available at plant or route level. The scenarios use exogenous aggregate multipliers and a constant-activity baseline, so they do not estimate costs, investments, market diffusion, policy feasibility or a full-IPPU forecast. The next step is a technology-explicit model based on national data for clinker-to-cement ratios, steel routes, scrap and reductant use, ferroalloy composition, N2O controls and carbon-capture availability. Thank you for your attention. I will be happy to take your questions.

## Anticipated questions and concise answers

| Likely question | Suggested answer |
|---|---|
| Why is this not a forecast of Kazakhstan’s full IPPU sector? | The model includes a selected set of process-emission branches. It excludes F-gases, aluminium PFCs, other minor IPPU sources and industrial fuel combustion, so it cannot be interpreted as a full-sector forecast. |
| Why are F-gases excluded when they are important historically? | They are important and should be represented in a future complete IPPU model. They were excluded here because the present LEAP implementation was designed around the selected mineral, metal and chemical process branches with verified input structure. |
| Are the Moderate and Ambitious percentages official Kazakhstan targets? | No. They are transparent, author-selected sensitivity assumptions that represent progressively deeper portfolios of recognised measures. |
| Why is the Baseline flat? | The flat Baseline isolates the effect of the stated emissions multipliers. It is not a projection of production growth or economic activity. |
| Can the CRF 2023 and CRT 2025 totals be directly compared? | CO2 can be compared more directly, but CO2-equivalent totals require caution because the submissions use AR4 and AR5 GWP values respectively. |
| Do matching BNS and CRT values prove that the data are fully accurate? | No. They show that the output indicators match. A full emissions estimate can still require route-specific parameters such as carbon balance, reductant use, alloy composition or lime type. |
| What is the main innovation of the study? | It treats activity-data comparability as a precondition for bottom-up scenario modelling and makes the boundary and assumptions of the LEAP scenarios fully transparent. |
